What a Water Damage Restoration Estimate Should Include

    24/7 emergency line(508) 286-8971Tap to call a dispatcher

    By the Stoughton Water Damage Pros team. Published November 14, 2026. Updated November 14, 2026.

    An estimate is only useful if you can compare it with another one. That means it has to say what will be done, with what, and for how long. A single total with no detail is a warning sign.

    No exact prices are given here, because they depend on the specific job.

    Scope of work

    The estimate should list the areas affected, the steps planned and the materials to be removed or kept. It should state whether extraction, drying and monitoring are included, and what is not included, such as rebuilding.

    Equipment and time

    Look for the number of air movers and dehumidifiers and the expected drying days. Equipment is often billed per day, so ask how the days are counted and who decides when equipment comes out.

    Line itemWhat to look forRed flag
    ScopeRooms and steps listedSingle lump sum
    EquipmentCount and daysNo days stated
    LaborTasks describedUnspecified hours
    Materials removedNamed materialsEverything gets cut
    DocumentationPhotos and readingsNone offered
    ExclusionsRebuilding listed as separateSilent on repairs

    Labor, materials and disposal

    Check that labor, removal of wet materials and disposal are described. If materials are cut out, the estimate should say how much and where. Ask whether a final amount can change and under what conditions.

    Insurance and documentation

    If an insurer is involved, ask for photos, moisture logs and a written scope. Insurers often require them. Ask whether the company will talk with the adjuster, and who is responsible for payment if coverage is denied.

    Questions that make an estimate clearer

    Ask what is included, what is excluded and what would change the total. Ask who decides when drying equipment comes out. Ask whether disposal fees are included. Ask if there is a minimum charge. Ask when you will receive photos and readings.

    Clear answers show that the company has thought about your job and not only the quote.

    • What is included and excluded?
    • What could change the total?
    • Who decides when equipment leaves?
    • Are disposal fees included?

    Comparing two estimates fairly

    Line the estimates up side by side. Check that they cover the same rooms and steps. A lower total may leave out equipment days or monitoring. A higher total may include rebuilding you did not ask for. Call both companies for clarification before choosing.

    What an estimate cannot tell you in advance

    Hidden damage behind walls, under floors or above ceilings may not show until materials are opened. A good estimate says so, and explains how changes will be handled. It may include a range or a first-phase scope with a plan to revisit.

    Be cautious of any estimate that claims certainty before anyone has taken readings.

    Paperwork to keep

    Keep the estimate, any signed agreement, photos, moisture logs, invoices and correspondence with your insurer in one folder. If something changes, ask for a written update. Good records protect you if there is a dispute or a claim question later.

    • Signed agreement and estimate
    • Photos and moisture logs
    • Invoices and receipts
    • Messages with your insurer

    Why apparent bargains can cost more

    A very low estimate may leave out drying equipment days, disposal or monitoring. When drying is cut short, mold can return, and the repair cost grows. A fair estimate costs what the work costs, and it explains why.

    Ask for a plain explanation of any line that surprises you.

    How to use an estimate after the job starts

    Check the work against the estimate each day. Ask the crew lead to explain any change before it happens. Keep the original document so you can see what was agreed. If your insurer needs a revised scope, ask the company to write one and send it to you first.

    Understanding drying equipment charges

    Equipment is commonly billed per day. A fair estimate states how many units are used and how many days are expected, and explains how extra days would be approved. Ask whether monitoring visits are included and who reads the meters. Clear answers prevent surprise charges.

    Estimates for different kinds of losses

    A clean supply line leak in one room produces a short, simple estimate. A multi-room flood with demolition produces a longer one with more line items. A sewage loss adds protective gear, disinfection and disposal. Expect the document to match the complexity of the job, and ask about anything that seems vague.

    What to Do Before You Accept an Estimate

    Work through these checks.

    • Confirm the estimate lists rooms, steps, equipment and days
    • Ask whether monitoring visits are included
    • Ask what could change the total
    • Ask for photos and moisture readings at the end

    Standards and Sources Behind This Page

    Estimate line items such as equipment days and monitoring reflect the drying process in the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration. Massachusetts requires registered contractors for home improvement work on owner-occupied one to four unit homes.
