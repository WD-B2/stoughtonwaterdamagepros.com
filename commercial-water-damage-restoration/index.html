Business downtime is part of the damage

A leak that hits a storage room, a server closet or a sales floor can stop revenue. Tenants and landlords also need to sort out responsibility fast.

Priorities in the first hour:

  • Protect electronics, records and inventory
  • Shut off supply and power to wet areas
  • Notify your landlord or property manager
  • Call your insurer and photograph the damage

Water sources in commercial buildings

Commercial properties see sprinkler head failures, rooftop unit leaks, supply line breaks, roof drains that back up and tenant-above leaks. Stoughton's Route 138 retail corridor and Town Center mix storefronts, offices and shared buildings, so one tenant's leak often reaches another.

Larger floor plates mean water travels farther before anyone notices.

Warning signs for commercial water damage

Facility managers should watch for these.

Ceiling tile stains

Brown tiles show repeated leaks above.

Damp carpet in offices

Carpet tiles hold moisture and smell.

Condensation around HVAC

Heavy condensation points to drain issues.

Unusual utility use

Meter increases with no change in operation can flag a hidden leak.

How we handle a commercial loss

Plans are shared with the property manager and insurer.

  1. Assess and protect

    The crew documents damage and protects contents.

  2. Extract and dry

    Pumps, extractors and commercial dehumidifiers go to work.

  3. Remove damaged materials

    Wet ceiling tiles, carpet and drywall are removed as needed.

  4. Report and coordinate

    Moisture logs and photos are provided for the insurer and owner.

What Property Managers Should Gather

Before the crew arrives, gather the lease clauses on water damage, your insurer's claim number if you have one, tenant contact details and the location of utility shutoffs. A floor plan helps the crew mark wet areas. Having these ready shortens the first meeting.

What to Do in the First Hour at a Business

Gather these steps for the first hour while the crew is assigned.

  • Shut off water to the affected area and the nearest breaker if safe
  • Lift inventory and records off wet floors
  • Notify the landlord, property manager and your insurer
  • Photograph the damage before anything is moved

Standards and Sources Behind This Page

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration applies to commercial structures as well as homes. It sets out water categories and drying classes that a crew uses to plan equipment, and it is a useful reference when a property manager asks how a drying plan was chosen.
